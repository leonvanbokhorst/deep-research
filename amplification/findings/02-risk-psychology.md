# The Psychological and Sociological Evidence Base for Engineered Risk Amplification

**Strand:** 02 — Risk psychology and the sociology of amplification
**Cut-off:** 25 September 2026
**Question:** Can social amplification of risk be deliberately engineered, and how manipulable are populations and institutions through *ambiguity*?
**Scope:** Analytic only. SARF describes accident and technology risk, not adversarial action; where that limits transfer it is stated explicitly.

**Confidence:** `[H]` high (replicated or primary/official); `[M]` moderate (credible scholarship, contested/unreplicated); `[L]` low (single-source or inferential). Method note: primary web search failed this session; discovery used news RSS, a DuckDuckGo text proxy and direct document retrieval.

## 1. Social Amplification of Risk Framework (SARF)

Kasperson, Renn, Slovic, Brown, Emel, Goble, Kasperson & Ratick (1988), *Risk Analysis* 8(2):177–187, proposed that a risk event generates an information signal that passes through **amplification stations** — media, scientists, agencies, advocacy groups, opinion leaders, personal networks — each intensifying, attenuating or reframing it, producing **ripple effects** across time, space, sectors and social groups (boycotts, litigation, regulation, stigma, lost trust). The central claim is that *secondary* social and economic impacts often exceed the direct physical harm. `[H]` <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x>

**Was it an engineering manual? No** — it was an integrative, descriptive framework whose policy implication was better risk communication and trust-building, not manipulation. `[H]` Three lines of evidence:

1. **Its critics attacked the engineering reading.** Rayner (1988), *Risk Analysis* 8(2):201–204, argued the amplification metaphor smuggles in a *true signal* that social processes distort, treats society as a passive transmission medium, and substitutes metaphor for mechanism. Rip (1988), same issue, asked whether amplification *should* be counteracted — treating the normative question as open. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01172.x> `[H]`
2. **Its own fifteen-year assessment conceded weak specification.** Kasperson, Kasperson, Pidgeon & Slovic (2003) in Pidgeon, Kasperson & Slovic (eds.), *The Social Amplification of Risk* (Cambridge UP), find amplification real but highly case-specific and better at post-hoc interpretation than prediction; Breakwell & Barnett's chapter concurs. <https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511550461> `[M]`
3. **Formalisation showed it is not falsifiable as a claim about objective risk.** Busby & Onggo (2013), *J. Operational Research Society* 64(5):638–653, modelled SARF and found that in contested cases there is no measurable true risk to be amplified — actors merely *attribute* amplification to each other. Once attributional dynamics are added, perceived risk polarises far more strongly (peak ratio ~40×, ~400× with distrust and confusion feedback) and **lingers indefinitely after the hazard ends**. <https://doi.org/10.1057/jors.2012.80> `[M]`

**Literature on *deliberate* use of amplification stations?** Not in SARF. The nearest thing is one category in Busby & Onggo's fieldwork: interviewees explained others' amplification via cognition, disposition, situation, **"strategy, or deliberate, instrumental action,"** and structure — deliberate amplification is recognised *as an attribution actors make*, not theorised as a capability. `[M]` The absence is structural: SARF has no variable for an adversary who *is* a station, who injects a false signal rather than relaying a real one, or who acts inside the ripple. A 2022 *Risk Analysis* collection ("New perspectives") revisits SARF for social media but supplies no offensive playbook. `[L]` on authorship, `[M]` on the absence.

**Transfer limits.** (a) No objective signal → success cannot be scored. (b) Stations are assumed independent and self-interested, not commanded. (c) Canonical cases had physical referents; adversarial ambiguity denies one. (d) SARF ripples are mostly unintended side-effects. Any claim that SARF predicts engineered amplification is an over-read.

## 2. Availability Cascades (Kuran & Sunstein 1999)

Kuran & Sunstein, "Availability Cascades and Risk Regulation," *Stanford Law Review* 51(4):683–768, describe a self-reinforcing cycle in which a belief gains currency because others hold it — the availability heuristic plus **reputational cascades** (public agreement pays better than accuracy). Key actors are **availability entrepreneurs** who invest to raise a risk's availability, plus journalists under competitive pressure, status-seeking politicians, and availability counter-entrepreneurs. Beliefs compete in an "availability market" for scarce attention. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1229478> `[H]`

Their cases — Love Canal, Alar, TWA Flight 800 — are real cascades. Sunstein's "Probability Neglect" (*Yale Law Journal* 112:61, 2002) supplies the engine: under strong emotion people respond to the *worst case*, not its likelihood, so a vivid, ambiguous, low-probability threat out-competes a statistically larger familiar one. `[H]`

**Exploitable?** Partially and asymmetrically. The entrepreneurs are documented and the market contested, but Kuran & Sunstein's remedies (risk-regulation committees, cost–benefit review, product-defamation liability, a Risk Information Center) presuppose *defensive* action — evidence they judged engineering something that happens *to* institutions rather than something institutions command. The same model also predicts fragility: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992), *JPE* 100(5):992–1026, show information cascades are highly sensitive to small shocks and can shatter as abruptly as they form. `[H]`

## 3. Moral Panic

Cohen (1972), *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*, established the concept: a condition, episode, person or group is defined as a threat to societal values; **moral entrepreneurs** drive the reaction; **folk devils** embody it; media create a **signification spiral** where each report confirms and escalates the last. Goode & Ben-Yehuda (1994) added an **attributional** model emphasising measurable disproportionality. Hall et al. (1978), *Policing the Crisis*, embedded panics in state legitimation crises.

**Lifecycle and duration.** Cohen's stages (emergence → media inventory and sensitisation → escalation/folk devil → experts and solutions → backlash/fade) describe cycles typically measured in **months to a few years**; the strongest documented cases (mods and rockers, satanic ritual abuse, 1980s Halloween candy-tampering, MMR–autism) held peak intensity for roughly two to six years — a pattern across case studies, not a measured distribution. `[M]`

**The engineering-relevant finding is the critic literature.** McRobbie & Thornton (1995), *British Journal of Sociology* 46(4):559–574, argue the model mis-specifies agency: in fragmented multi-mediated environments there is no single controlling definer, panics are discontinuous, and audiences read campaigns **ironically and reflexively** rather than being captured. Waddington (1986) showed the proportionality test fails without a baseline; Critcher (2008) notes the concept became so elastic it now covers almost any public worry, degrading its predictive value. <https://doi.org/10.2307/591571> `[H]`

**Implication:** moral panic is recurrent and real, but the scholarship's consistent message is that panics *emerge* from structural strain and are then *narrated*. Where origins have been traced to identifiable entrepreneurs (Halloween candy, satanic abuse), the entrepreneurs amplified and sustained a panic already underway, and the harm claims were false. Deliberate origination at scale is undocumented.

## 4. Rumour Psychology

Allport & Postman (1947), *The Psychology of Rumor*, proposed the basic law **R ~ i × a** — rumour intensity varies with the *importance* of the subject and the *ambiguity* of the evidence. Ambiguity is the multiplier an adversary can most plausibly move: importance is largely fixed by subject matter; ambiguity is a property of the information environment. They also documented **levelling**, **sharpening** and **assimilation** (distortion toward prior belief). `[H]` on canonical content, `[M]` on precision — the multiplicative form was never robustly estimated. Rosnow (1991), *American Psychologist* 46(5):484–496, reformulated it as R = i × a × c, separating certainty, and argued ambiguity drives *both* sensemaking and anxiety-driven transmission. `[H]`

Shibutani (1966), *Improvised News*, reframed rumour as **collective sensemaking under information scarcity**. DiFonzo & Bordia (2007), *Rumor Psychology* (APA), concluded transmission is driven mainly by *epistemic* motives (wanting accurate information) and *social* motives (belonging, self-enhancement), with anxiety a moderator rather than the primary driver. <https://doi.org/10.1037/11503-000> `[M]`

**Consequence:** ambiguity reliably *starts* rumour, but recipients reshape content toward prior belief and actively seek disconfirmation. An adversary controls the ambiguity, not the interpretation, nor whether a crowd treats the gap as a puzzle or a threat. First strong hint of the emergent view.

## 5. Risk Perception: Dread, Unknown, Signal Value

Slovic (1987), "Perception of Risk," *Science* 236(4799):280–285, using the psychometric paradigm from Fischhoff, Slovic, Lichtenstein, Read & Combs (1978), *Policy Sciences* 9(2):127–152, identified two orthogonal factors. **Dread risk** covers catastrophic potential, uncontrollability, involuntariness, inequitable distribution, threat to future generations, and above all **unobservability, unknown-to-exposed, delayed effects**. **Unknown risk** covers newness, unfamiliarity and whether science understands it.

**Signal value** (Slovic, Fischhoff & Lichtenstein, 1982) is load-bearing here: an event's *informational* content about future, possibly worse events — not its casualties — drives response. Chernobyl produced this effect through mere implied comparability. `[H]`

Two asymmetries matter. **Amplification is easier than attenuation:** Gregory, Flynn & Slovic (1995), "Technological stigma," *American Behavioral Scientist* 38(8):1107–1113, show negative associations persist and resist reassurance, and trust is quick to lose, slow to regain. `[H]` **Emotion crowds out probability:** Loewenstein, Weber, Hsee & Welch (2001), "Risk as feelings," *Psychological Bulletin* 127(2):267–286, and Slovic et al.'s affect heuristic show judgement shifts from expected value to feeling under vividness and time pressure — exactly why ambiguous, invisible, unfamiliar threats produce disproportionate response. `[H]`

**Countervailing psychophysiology:** "Acute Stress Reduces the Social Amplification of Risk Perception" (*Scientific Reports*, May 2020) found induced acute stress *reduced* social amplification of risk judgement. `[M]` If it reproduces, peak-crisis conditions make people *less* socially suggestible about risk — a direct problem for panic-engineering theory.

## 6. The Engineering Question

**Shock advertising — weak, self-limiting.** Dahl, Frankenberger & Manchanda (2003), *J. Advertising Research* 43(3):268–280, found shock appeals capture attention but give **no reliable recall or persuasion advantage** and risk negative brand attitude; Vézina & Paul (1997) and Parry et al. (2013) report the same attention-without-attitude-change pattern. `[M]`

**Agenda-setting and outrage.** McCombs & Shaw (1972), *Public Opinion Quarterly* 36(2):176–187, established that media salience transfers to public salience — replicated, but it transfers *issue importance*, not belief content or behaviour. Berry & Sobieraj (2014), *The Outrage Industry*, documents a commercial ecosystem that reliably manufactures attention and affective polarisation while conceding limited persuasion. Kalla & Broockman's (2018) meta-analysis of 49 field experiments, *APSR* 112(1):148–166, found the **average persuasive effect of campaign contact indistinguishable from zero**. `[H]` **"Dead cat"** agenda displacement is well attested and consistent with agenda-setting under attention scarcity, but the literature is journalistic; the effect is displacement among already-engaged elites, not panic induction. `[M]`

**State and military influence operations.**

- **RAND's "firehose of falsehood"** (Paul & Matthews, 2016, PE-198) characterises the Russian model as high-volume, multi-channel, rapid, continuous and **lacking commitment to objective reality or consistency**. RAND's assessment is that this obscures and exhausts rather than installing specific beliefs — cheap, not reliably causal. <https://www.rand.org/pubs/perspectives/PE198.html> `[M]`
- **Reflexive control** (Thomas, 2004, *J. Slavic Military Studies* 17(2):237–256) is the closest thing to an explicit doctrine of manipulating an adversary by feeding its priors back to it, but it targets **command decision-makers**, not mass populations, and Western literature is largely interpretive reconstruction. `[M]` doctrine, `[L]` demonstrated efficacy.
- **EEAS, 4th Report on FIMI Threats (March 2026)** documents deliberate amplification as an industrial, layered architecture — production, proxies, "amplification networks," monetisation — and frames AI as making it cheaper and more scalable. This is the best current primary evidence that *amplification infrastructure* is deliberately built; it is a threat assessment, not an effect-size study, and claims no measured panic creation. `[M]`

**Measured effects of state campaigns are near zero.** Eady, Paskhalis, Zilinsky, Bonneau, Nagler & Tucker (2023), *Nature Communications* 14:62, linked survey respondents to Russian Internet Research Agency exposure in 2016: relationships with issue positions and perceived polarisation were **≈0.05–0.06 SD**, rejecting effects larger than 0.2 SD; estimated effect on Trump vote **−0.18 pp (90% CI −1.15, +0.78)**. Bail et al. (2020), *PNAS* 117(1):243–250, concur. <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9> `[H]`

**Documented deliberate framing — and why it instructs.** The 1990 Nayirah testimony (a coached 15-year-old giving fabricated testimony about Iraqi soldiers killing Kuwaiti infants, placed by Hill & Knowlton at the highest amplification station) is a clear admitted case. It moved congressional votes — but only inside an **already-mobilised war coalition**, and was exposed within two years by the same media system. `[H]` The contrast case is the 1938 *War of the Worlds* broadcast, cited as proof media can trigger mass panic. Pooley & Socolow (Slate, 28 Oct 2013) used contemporaneous Hooper ratings to show the panic was largely a **newspaper-constructed story about radio**; Cantril's (1940) *The Invasion from Mars* found most listeners did not panic, and those who did had pre-existing vulnerabilities. `[M]`

**Why deliberate panic creation is hard.**

- **Cry-wolf / alarm fatigue.** Breznitz (1984), *Cry Wolf*. `[H]` *But* Barnes et al. (2007), *Meteorological Applications* 14(4):345–352, and Dow & Cutter (1998), *Coastal Management* 26(4):237–252, found false alarms did **not** meaningfully reduce compliance with tornado warnings and hurricane evacuations. Cry-wolf is real in bounded technical settings, weaker for safety warnings. `[M]`
- **Habituation.** Busby & Onggo's model includes a natural relaxation flow out of the worried state: *it is hard to stay worried about a specific issue for any length of time*. `[M]`
- **Psychic numbing.** Slovic (2007), *Judgment and Decision Making* 2(2):79–95 — response to mass suffering *declines* as scale increases, so maximising ambiguous victims may suppress rather than amplify response.
- **Inoculation and prebunking.** McGuire (1961); Banas & Rains (2010), *Communication Monographs* 77(3):281–311; Roozenbeek & van der Linden (2019), *Palgrave Communications* 5:65; Roozenbeek et al. (2022), *Science Advances* 8(34):eabo6254 — gamified inoculation improves discernment at population scale, and 2026 video prebunking trials span 12 EU nations. `[H]` Once a population is inoculated against a *technique*, re-use decays.
- **Cascade fragility.** Bikhchandani et al. (1992), above. `[H]`

## 7. Crisis Informatics: What Actually Happens in Emergencies

Starbird, Maddock, Orand, Achterman & Mason (2014), iConference 2014, analysed three false rumours after the 2013 Boston Marathon bombing. Corrections emerged but were **muted and lagged** relative to misinformation at ratios of **18:1, 18:1 and 5:1**; the two rise and fall together with "exponentially different amplitude." The "digital vigilante" rumour misidentifying **Sunil Tripathi** was driven by **well-meaning citizens** trying to help, amplified on Reddit and briefly by professional media. Only an **official announcement** terminated it. <https://faculty.washington.edu/kstarbi/Starbird_iConference2014-final.pdf> `[H]` Maddock et al. (2015), CSCW, extend this to signature patterns over time; Oh, Agrawal & Rao (2013), *MIS Quarterly* 37(2):407–426, found rumour-correcting and sensemaking tweets a substantial, mostly self-organising share of crisis traffic: the crowd polices itself, slowly and incompletely. `[M]`

**The decisive counter-evidence.** Quarantelli & Dynes (1977), *Annual Review of Sociology* 3:23–49; Clarke (2002), "Panic: Myth or Reality?", *Contexts* 1(3):21–26; Drury, Cocking & Reicher (2009) on the 2005 London bombings; Tierney, Bevc & Kuligowski (2006), *Annals AAPSS* 604:57–81 — the consistent finding is that **mass panic in disasters is rare to non-existent**; survivors behave prosocially and orderly; the "panic" narrative is largely a **media frame** that then drives counterproductive policy (the Katrina "snipers and rapes" myths). Helsloot & Ruitenberg (2004) concur. `[H]`

This is the most damaging body of evidence for engineerable panic: the real emergencies where panic should be easiest to produce are exactly where it is least observed.

## 8. Are People Actually Credulous?

**Backfire and its partial retraction.** Nyhan & Reifler (2010), *Political Behavior* 32(2):303–330, found corrections could entrench misperceptions. Wood & Porter (2019), *Political Behavior* 41(1):135–163, ran four experiments with >8,100 subjects across **36 potential backfire issues** and found **one** (WMD in Iraq), which vanished with a less elaborate item: "by and large, citizens heed factual information." <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y> `[H]` Nyhan (2021), *PNAS* 118(15):e1912440117, argues backfire does not explain durable misperception; persistence reflects motivated reasoning and identity protection. Swire-Thompson, DeGutis & Lazer (2020) show much apparent backfire is a measurement artefact. `[H]` The retraction is **partial**: corrections rarely backfire but also rarely fully correct — Walter & Tukachinsky (2020), *Communication Research* 47(2):155–177, meta-analytically confirm a **continued influence effect**. `[H]`

**Exposure is small, concentrated, and not persuasion.** Guess, Nagler & Tucker (2019), *Science Advances* 5(1):eaau4586, found fake-news sharing heavily concentrated in a small minority, most users never sharing any; Allcott & Gentzkow (2017), *JEP* 31(2):211–236, found the average American saw very few false stories; Guess, Nyhan & Reifler (2020), *Nature Human Behaviour* 4:472–480, found untrustworthy-site exposure concentrated among an already-extreme minority; Boxell, Gentzkow & Shapiro (2017), *PNAS* 114(40):10612–10617, found greater internet use was **not** associated with faster growth in political polarisation. `[H]`

**Net reading:** the modal citizen is neither a Bayesian nor a dupe, but a *motivated* reasoner with low attention, weak priors on most topics and strong priors on a few. That yields high **variance** and low **commandability**: the same stimulus lands very differently across groups — fatal to engineered, population-wide effects.

## 9. Verdict

**The emergent view is substantially better supported.** Ambiguity amplification is best modelled as an *emergent, attributional, self-organising* phenomenon in which agents supply sparks and exploit pre-existing fuel but cannot control ignition, propagation, or duration.

The engineering case rests on real foundations: availability entrepreneurs exist and invest; agenda-setting does transfer salience; moral panics recur; inoculation research shows the same channels are attackable; and EEAS's 2026 FIMI reporting confirms that *amplification infrastructure* — networks, proxies, monetisation, AI scaling — is deliberately built. That infrastructure is genuine. `[H]`

But measured attempts to convert it into population-level belief or behaviour change come back near zero: Eady et al.'s 0.05–0.06 SD and vote effects inside ±1 pp; Kalla & Broockman's zero average persuasion across 49 experiments; shock advertising's attention without persuasion; the *War of the Worlds* panic that was largely a media myth. The mechanisms that would have to be controlled are held by actors the engineer does not command: journalists competing for attention, partisans protecting identity, well-meaning citizens doing crisis sensemaking (Starbird's digital vigilantes), and platforms optimising engagement rather than message.

**Strongest counter-evidence, at full strength:**

1. **SARF's attributional critique cuts both ways.** Once actors attribute amplification to each other, perceived risk polarises hundreds-fold and **does not decay** — indefinite anxiety, distrust and depressed activity, with no deliberate actor needed. If a small injection can tip an attributional dynamic into a self-sustaining state, the practical gap between "emergent" and "engineered" narrows sharply.
2. **Institutional cascades ARE engineerable even when public cascades are not.** The load-bearing correction. In the Havana syndrome case (2016–2026, still live in September 2026 with a Pentagon device acquisition under investigation), a decade of unresolved ambiguity produced task forces, legislation, compensation, intelligence-community reorganisation and continuing procurement — with contested evidence of mass public panic. The asymmetry is the finding: **elites and institutions are far more manipulable through ambiguity than populations are.** Kasperson's ripple includes institutional ripple, and that is where the leverage sits.
3. **Sustained low-grade ambiguity beats acute panic induction.** Measured failures mostly targeted beliefs or votes. Ambiguity's real return may be eroding institutional trust, exhausting investigative capacity, and shifting the null hypothesis — "we cannot rule out a deliberate attack" — cheap to maintain, hard to falsify. That is a slower, less visible and more plausible objective than panic.

**Bottom line:** population-level panic is not reliably engineerable, and the evidence against it is strong and convergent. Institutional-level amplification, and the maintenance of open-ended ambiguity, is materially more engineerable — but it operates through *emergent* uptake by journalists, advocates and officials, not command. Verdict: **emergent with a controllable foothold** — agents can reliably influence *whether an ambiguity is noticed and sustained by institutions*; they cannot reliably determine *what a population does with it*.

## Where SARF Does Not Transfer (explicit)

SARF's domains are accident and technology hazards with a physical referent: nuclear, chemical, EMF, contaminated products, zoonoses. Its stations are assumed independent, self-interested and non-commandable. It therefore cannot represent: (a) an adversary acting *as* a station; (b) injection of a wholly synthetic signal with no referent; (c) a target chosen because it is *unfalsifiable*; (d) success measured in institutional attention rather than physical harm. Any downstream analysis citing SARF as authority for deliberate-amplification capability is misusing it. The adversarial evidence here comes from influence-operations research, crisis informatics and political communication — not SARF.
