# Cascade Theory & Amplification — Source Audit

**Scope.** Canonical citations, actual claims, and critiques for five literatures bearing on the hypothesis of *agentic threat amplification* (AI agents deliberately amplifying ambiguous physical incidents so that communities, media and institutions generate the disruptive effect themselves).

**Method note.** `web_search` was unavailable/broken. Sources were retrieved via direct URLs, repository landing pages, `web_fetch`, Crossref, Open Library full-text search (`/search/inside.json`), and archive.org full-text. Where I could not open the primary text I say so. Confidence: **H** = primary text/authoritative record checked; **M** = canonical citation + widely-reported content; **L** = weak/indirect.

**Hypothesis (context only).** Novelty claim: (i) AI agents as a cheap, autonomous, scalable amplification engine; (ii) targeting *ambiguous physical incidents* rather than beliefs/opinion; (iii) the target population/institution supplies the disruptive effect.

## A) Rumour cascades and rumour theory

**Canonical sources.**
- Allport, G. W., & Postman, L. (1947). *The Psychology of Rumor*. New York: Henry Holt. Full text: https://ia601502.us.archive.org/24/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.268464/2015.268464.The-Psychology_djvu.txt (item https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.268464).
- Chorus, A. (1953). "The basic law of rumor." *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* 48(2), 313–314. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0060600 (see also Krech's 1948 review, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0051085; Rosnow 1991, https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.46.5.484; Miller 2005, https://doi.org/10.1525/si.2005.28.4.505).
- Shibutani, T. (1966). *Improvised News: A Sociological Study of Rumor*. Indianapolis: Bobbs-Merrill. https://openlibrary.org/works/OL5104312W.
- Sunstein, C. R. (2009/2014). *On Rumors*. https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400851225; Sunstein & Vermeule (2009), "Conspiracy Theories: Causes and Cures," *Journal of Political Philosophy* 17(2), 202–227, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9760.2008.00325.x.
- DiFonzo, N., & Bordia, P. (2007). *Rumor Psychology: Social and Organizational Approaches*. APA. https://doi.org/10.1037/11503-000.
- Starbird, K., Maddock, J., Orand, M., Achterman, P., & Mason, R. M. (2014). "Rumors, False Flags, and Digital Vigilantes: Misinformation on Twitter after the 2013 Boston Marathon Bombing." *iConference 2014*, 654–662. https://doi.org/10.9776/14308 — PDF: https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/47268/bitstreams/138716/data.pdf.
- Friggeri, A., Adamic, L., Eckles, D., & Cheng, J. (2014). "Rumor Cascades." *ICWSM* 8(1). https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v8i1.14559 — PDF: https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/14559/14408.

**What it claims.** Allport & Postman's "basic law," verbatim: *"the amount of rumor in circulation will vary with the importance of the subject to the individuals concerned times the ambiguity of the evidence pertaining to the topic at issue. The relation between importance and ambiguity is not additive but multiplicative, for if either importance or ambiguity is zero, there is no rumor"* (pp. 33–34) **[H]**. Chorus (1953) is the standard later revision, adding a "critical sense"/credulity factor to the multiplicative formula **[M — citation H, content M, primary paywalled]**. Allport himself listed conditions weakening the law: surveillance and penalties, socially heterogeneous populations that block transmission, and "rumor-wise" self-awareness that can negate the prediction (p. 35) **[H]**. The law is now widely treated as weak/contested and as never adequately validated; contemporaneous and later critiques include Krech's 1948 review, Rosnow (1991) and Miller (2005) **[M — citations H, critique content mostly UNVERIFIED behind paywalls]**.

Shibutani is the key alternative framing: rumour is *collective problem-solving/sensemaking under ambiguity*, not pathology. Page 282 contains, verified by full-text phrase search: *"men caught together in an ambiguous situation attempt to construct a meaningful interpretation of it by pooling their intellectual resources"* **[H]**. (The opening clause usually rendered "Rumor is a recurrent form of communication…" did **not** match the scan — UNVERIFIED.)

**Audience as amplifier — established.** Yes, explicitly. Allport & Postman ran serial-transmission experiments with and without spectators: *"the terminal protocols were shorter and on the whole less accurate when obtained with an audience than when obtained from subjects making their reports only to the experimenter"* and *"though the rate of leveling is speeded up with an audience, the phenomenon itself is universal"* (pp. 73–74, 80) **[H]**. Modern evidence: Starbird et al. report misinformation-to-correction ratios of ~44:1, 18:1 and ~5:1 and conclude *"the notion of the self-correcting crowd may be overly optimistic"*; corrections *"emerge but are muted compared with the propagation of the misinformation"* and *"the amplitude can be exponentially different"* **[H]**. Friggeri et al.: *"rumor cascades run deeper in the social network than reshare cascades in general"* and *"large cascades are able to accumulate hundreds of Snopes comments while continuing to propagate"* **[H]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Emergent, with a deliberate minority. Starbird et al. explicitly distinguish *"purposeful misinformation, introduced by someone who knew it to be false, and accidental misinformation, often caused by lost context"* **[H]**. The dominant tradition (Shibutani; DiFonzo & Bordia) models rumour as bottom-up sensemaking.

**Fit: Partial (mechanism High, targeting Partial).** Strong fit for (iii) — the audience/institution demonstrably supplies amplification and correction is weak — and moderate fit for (ii), since rumour theory concerns ambiguous *information*, and its trigger is often an ambiguous event. **Where it does NOT fit:** rumour research targets beliefs/opinion and information deficits, not physical incidents; it treats the process as emergent, not engineered; and its key variable (ambiguity reduction) is the opposite of an adversary trying to *sustain* ambiguity.

## B) Moral panic

**Canonical sources.**
- Cohen, S. (1972). *Folk Devils and Moral Panics: The Creation of the Mods and Rockers*. London: MacGibbon & Kee (Routledge 2002/2011 eds.). https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203828250.
- Goode, E., & Ben-Yehuda, N. (1994). *Moral Panics: The Social Construction of Deviance*. Oxford: Blackwell; and "Moral Panics: Culture, Politics, and Social Construction," *Annual Review of Sociology* 20, 149–171, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.so.20.080194.001053.
- Garland, D. (2008). "On the concept of moral panic." *Crime, Media, Culture* 4(1), 9–30. https://doi.org/10.1177/1741659007087270.
- McRobbie, A., & Thornton, S. L. (1995). "Rethinking 'Moral Panic' for Multi-Mediated Social Worlds." *British Journal of Sociology* 46(4), 559–574. https://doi.org/10.2307/591571.
- Altheide, D. L. (2009). "Moral panic: From sociological concept to public discourse." *Crime, Media, Culture* 5(1), 79–99. https://doi.org/10.1177/1741659008102063. Innes (2005), https://doi.org/10.1177/1741659005050263.

**What it claims.** Cohen's classic definition (p. 9) — a condition/episode/person/group is *"defined as a threat to societal values and interests"*, presented *"in a stylized and stereotypical fashion by the mass media"*, with *"moral barricades… manned by editors, bishops, politicians and other right-thinking people"*, experts pronouncing diagnoses, after which the condition *"disappears, submerges or deteriorates"* **[M — canonical and universally quoted, but I could not open the scan to verify page-level wording]**. Key roles: **folk devil**, **moral entrepreneur**, **control culture**; the **deviancy amplification spiral** (the amplification idea descends from Wilkins' "deviancy amplification," developed by Jock Young and Cohen) **[M]**. Goode & Ben-Yehuda's five defining attributes — **concern, hostility, consensus, disproportionality, volatility** — and three explanatory theories — **grassroots, elite-engineered, interest-group** — are their core contribution. Their verified *Annual Review* abstract states panics are constructed, that concern *"need not bear a close relationship with the concrete harm"*, that folk devils are *"stereotyped and classified as deviants"*, and that *"moral panics always leave an informal, and often an institutional, legacy"* **[H for the abstract; M for the five-attribute list]**.

**Critiques.** Cohen's own later prefaces (2002/2011) are widely reported to warn the concept has been overused and risks becoming a cliché; I could **not** verify the exact wording — **UNVERIFIED**. Garland (2008) argues the label has become a rhetorical device applied too freely rather than an analytic concept **[M — citation H, content M]**. McRobbie & Thornton (1995) argue the model's assumptions — a single national media, a passive audience, a state-centred "control culture" — break down in multi-mediated worlds **[M]**. Altheide (2009) documents the concept's migration into public discourse and loss of precision **[M]**. The net critique: elasticity — any strong public reaction can be labelled a panic, so the concept risks explaining nothing **[M]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Both are theorized, and this is the concept's most useful feature for the hypothesis: Goode & Ben-Yehuda name an **elite-engineered** model alongside grassroots and interest-group models **[H]**.

**Fit: Partial.** The amplification spiral and "moral entrepreneur" role model exactly the mechanism by which an ambiguous trigger plus institutional reaction produces disproportionate disruption, and the elite-engineered variant licenses deliberate engineering. **Where it does NOT fit:** moral panic requires a *folk devil* and a normative/deviance threat; the hypothesis targets ambiguous physical hazards without a deviant out-group. The literature is also overwhelmingly about emergent, media-driven reactions, and its critics warn against using the label for every strong response.

## C) Availability cascades

**Canonical source.** Kuran, T., & Sunstein, C. R. (1999). "Availability Cascades and Risk Regulation." *Stanford Law Review* 51(4), 683–768. https://doi.org/10.2307/1229439. Open access: Chicago Unbound working paper, https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/public_law_and_legal_theory/37 ; SSRN, https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=138144. Journal/volume/pages confirmed by Kuran's own CV.

**What it claims (verbatim from the authors' abstract) [H].** *"An availability cascade is a self-reinforcing process of collective belief formation by which an expressed perception triggers a chain reaction that gives the perception increasing plausibility through its rising availability in public discourse."* Mechanism: *"a combination of informational and reputational motives: Individuals endorse the perception partly by learning from the apparent beliefs of others and partly by distorting their public responses in the interest of maintaining social acceptance."* The term **"availability entrepreneurs"** is theirs: *"activists who manipulate the content of public discourse—strive to trigger availability cascades likely to advance their agendas."* They argue such campaigns *"may yield social benefits, but sometimes they bring harm"*, and propose insulating civil servants from mass demands and an accessible scientific database **[H]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Explicitly dual: informational/reputational cascades are emergent, while availability entrepreneurs are deliberate triggers. The article's whole point is that *engineered* triggering is possible and consequential **[H]**.

**Critiques.** There is no single canonical refutation that I could locate — the critique is diffuse. Recurring lines are that the model is hard to falsify and is typically demonstrated by case study rather than tested; that "availability entrepreneur" is a residual category that can be asserted post hoc; and that it underplays organized interest groups and structural media incentives **[M–L, and partly UNVERIFIED — I could not retrieve a dedicated critique article]**. Note Kuran & Sunstein themselves later extended the argument ("Controlling Availability Cascades," in Sunstein ed., *Behavioral Law and Economics*, 2000, https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9781139175197.016).

**Fit: High for (i) and (iii); Partial overall.** This is the closest theoretical match to "deliberate amplification by agenda-driven actors whose target's own beliefs and need for social acceptance supply the escalation." **Where it does NOT fit:** the currency is *availability in public discourse* and *belief*, not physical incidents; the model's remedy is epistemic (expert insulation, data), not about operational disruption; and it says little about how ambiguous physical events specifically propagate.

## D) Social Amplification of Risk Framework (SARF)

**Canonical sources.**
- Kasperson, R. E., Renn, O., Slovic, P., Brown, H. S., Emel, J., Goble, R., Kasperson, J. X., & Ratick, S. (1988). "The Social Amplification of Risk: A Conceptual Framework." *Risk Analysis* 8(2), 177–187. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x — record https://commons.clarku.edu/faculty_idce/195 ; full text (Wayback copy of OA deposit) https://web.archive.org/web/20241010151234/https://elib.uni-stuttgart.de/bitstream/11682/7238/1/ren27.pdf.
- Pidgeon, N., Kasperson, R. E., & Slovic, P. (eds.) (2003). *The Social Amplification of Risk*. Cambridge UP. https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511550461.
- Kasperson, R. E., Webler, T., Ram, B., & Sutton, J. (2022). "The social amplification of risk framework: New perspectives." *Risk Analysis* 42(7), 1367–1380. https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.13926 (OA: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360138/).

**What it claims (verbatim, primary text) [H].** *"Social amplification of risk denotes the phenomenon by which information processes, institutional structures, social-group behavior, and individual responses shape the social experience of risk, thereby contributing to risk consequences."* Amplification occurs *"in the transfer of information about the risk, and in the response mechanisms of society"*; signals are processed by *"individual and social amplification stations, including the scientist who communicates the risk assessment, the news media, cultural groups, interpersonal networks, and others"* (seven stations). The metaphor: *"amplification denotes the process of intensifying or attenuating signals during the transmission of information from an information source, to intermediate transmitters, and finally to a receiver."* Ripple effects: *"The ripples spread outward, first encompassing the directly affected victims… then touching the next higher institutional level (a company or an agency), and, in more extreme cases, reaching other parts of the industry."* Four response pathways: heuristics/values, social-group relationships, signal value, stigmatization. The authors self-limit it as *"a fledgling conceptual framework"* **[H]**.

**Revisions.** Pidgeon et al. (2003) add metatheory, organizational amplification, trust, a media rethink, and policy analysis **[H for TOC, M for content]**. The 2022 review surveys 50+ post-2003 studies, foregrounds social media as a station, and concludes SARF is *"not a predictive theory of social outcomes"* but an organizational aid **[H]**.

**Critiques.** Critiques began in the **same issue**: Rayner, "Muddling Through Metaphors to Maturity" (1988), https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01172.x, plus Rip, Rappaport and Svenson commentaries **[H existence; M content — paywalled]**. The 2022 review concedes: *"Some critics suggest that SARF cannot be tested thus, the framework is useful, at most, as a post hoc analysis of some kinds of risks"*, and debates *"whether SARF is limited as a framework and a metaphor and is not a theory"* **[H]**. So the charges — descriptive not predictive, metaphor vague/untestable, thin empirical support — are acknowledged by its own architects.

**Deliberate/adversarial amplification — key negative finding.** Extensive exact-phrase full-text and title searches found **no established connection** between SARF and deliberate/adversarial amplification: "adversarial social amplification of risk," "deliberate amplification of risk," "weaponised/weaponized social amplification," "intentional amplification of risk" returned zero substantive hits; the 2022 review's survey contains no adversarial framing **[M–H for absence; search tools were partly bot-blocked, so not exhaustive]**. Adjacent work: Fjaeran & Aven (2021) propose *intentionally injecting* amplification to counter attenuation (defensive), https://doi.org/10.1080/13669877.2019.1687579 ; Ram & Webler (2022) document advocacy strategies to influence risk perceptions, https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.13902 . Deliberate amplification is theorized in disinformation/hybrid-warfare work but **not bridged to SARF** **[M]**. This is a genuine gap the hypothesis could claim.

**Fit: High for mechanism, Partial for deliberate.** SARF is the best available model of exactly (iii): signals pass through "stations" (media, institutions, networks) that co-produce behavioural and secondary impacts from a modest trigger. **Where it does NOT fit:** it is explicitly emergent and non-predictive; it has no adversary; and it is information/risk-perception-centred, not incident-centred. Any claim that SARF *already* covers adversarial amplification would be unsupported.

## E) Information laundering

**Canonical sources.**
- Peterson, D. J. (2005). *Russia and the Information Revolution*. RAND MG-422, p. 84. https://www.rand.org/pubs/monographs/MG422.html (PDF: https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/monographs/2005/RAND_MG422.pdf).
- Klein, A. G. (2010). *A Space for Hate*. Litwin Books; and Klein, A. (2012). "Slipping Racism into the Mainstream: A Theory of Information Laundering." *Communication Theory* 22(4), 427–448. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2012.01415.x.
- Puschmann, C., Ausserhofer, J., Maan, N., & Hametner, M. (2016). "Information Laundering and Counter-Publics: The News Sources of Islamophobic Groups on Twitter." *ICWSM* 10(2). https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v10i2.14847.
- DFRLab usage, e.g. https://dfrlab.org/2017/01/12/three-thousand-fake-tanks/.

**What it claims.** The consistent core: move content through intermediaries (front groups, fringe sites, fake personas, legitimate outlets) so provenance is obscured and borrowed credibility accrues. RAND (2005), verbatim: *"'information laundering'—posting rumors or gossip, which then would be picked up by the mainstream broadcast and print media as legitimate stories"* **[H]**. Puschmann et al., verbatim, following Klein: *"Information laundering… is the successful redressing of racist ideology in the guise of legitimate information, achieved by relying on the same forms, appearances and labels that give traditional information their societal legitimacy"* **[H]**. DFRLab describes a story launched by a dubious site, picked up by fringe sites, then repeated by RIA Novosti using those sites as the source — *"a classic case of disinformation laundering"* **[H]**.

**Origin.** Earlier and more diffuse than usually assumed: the earliest verified use is Ed Ayres, *God's Last Offer* (1999); the earliest defence/IO-adjacent use is RAND 2005; the academic theory is Klein (2010 book; 2012 article) **[H/M]**. Usage is **contested and loose**: incompatible senses appear for PR humour, pharma ghost-journals, industry front groups, Russian kompromat, and racist propaganda **[H]**. **UNVERIFIED:** any 2003/2007 US military IO-doctrine usage (DTIC search found none); any Matusitz usage; a "cyber-laundering" lineage (that literature is a financial-crime metaphor).

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Predominantly **deliberate engineering** (Klein, RAND, DFRLab) **[H]**; Puschmann et al.'s source-overlap operationalisation also captures partly emergent, systemic amplification **[M]**.

**Fit: High for (i), Partial overall.** Directly models deliberate, cheap, intermediary-mediated amplification, and the "audience/institution supplies credibility" step maps onto (iii). **Where it does NOT fit:** it concerns *information/belief provenance*, not physical incidents; it stops at credibility transfer and does not model operational disruption or audience-generated effects; and its definitions are too loose to be operationalised without care.

## Cross-cutting verdict

| Literature | Deliberate? | Audience amplifies? | Physical incidents? | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rumour theory (Allport; Shibutani; Starbird) | Emergent (minor deliberate) | **Yes — established [H]** | Partial (ambiguous information) | **Partial** |
| Moral panic (Cohen; Goode & Ben-Yehuda) | Both (elite-engineered variant) | Yes (media/institutions) | No (deviance framing) | **Partial** |
| Availability cascades (Kuran & Sunstein) | **Yes — entrepreneurs [H]** | Yes (belief/reputation) | No | **High/Partial** |
| SARF (Kasperson et al.) | **No — explicitly not** | **Yes — stations/ripples [H]** | **Yes — risk events [H]** | **High/Partial** |
| Information laundering | **Yes [H]** | Yes (credibility transfer) | No | **High/Partial** |

**Bottom line for the hypothesis.** The claim that rumour/risk theory treats the audience as amplifier is **well supported [H]**. The claim that AI agents are a novel amplification *engine* is **not contradicted** by any of these literatures — none models autonomous, scalable, agentic amplifiers. The "ambiguous physical incident" target is **only partially covered** (SARF handles risk events but assumes no adversary; rumour theory handles ambiguity but about information). The "adversarial/deliberate" bridge is **absent from SARF specifically [M–H negative finding]** and present only in availability-cascade and information-laundering work. The strongest novelty claim is therefore the *synthesis*: deliberate amplification (availability cascades, information laundering) of *ambiguous physical risk events* (SARF's domain) via autonomous agents, with the audience supplying the disruptive effect (rumour theory/moral panic).

**Residual uncertainty.** Paywalled and unverified: Cohen's exact 2002 preface wording; Garland 2008 and McRobbie & Thornton 1995 argument details; Chorus 1953's exact added factor; Miller 2005 and Krech 1948 critique content; Goode & Ben-Yehuda's five attributes as stated in the 1994 book (the *Annual Review* abstract is verified but does not list them); Rayner 1988 verbatim text. Search-engine access was largely bot-blocked, so the negative finding on "adversarial SARF" is strong but not exhaustive.
