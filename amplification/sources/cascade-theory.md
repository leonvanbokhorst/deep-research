# Cascade Theory & Amplification — Source Audit

**Scope.** Canonical citations, claims and critiques for five literatures bearing on *agentic threat amplification*: (i) AI agents as a cheap, autonomous, scalable amplification engine; (ii) targeting *ambiguous physical incidents* rather than beliefs/opinion; (iii) the target population/institution supplying the disruptive effect.

**Method.** `web_search` was broken. Sources came from direct URLs, repository pages, `web_fetch`, Crossref, Open Library full-text search (`/search/inside.json`), archive.org and RAND/DFRLab. Confidence: **H** = primary/authoritative record checked; **M** = canonical citation + widely-reported content; **L** = weak/indirect. Unopened primary texts are flagged.

## A) Rumour cascades and rumour theory

**Sources.** Allport & Postman, *The Psychology of Rumor* (Henry Holt, 1947) — full text https://ia601502.us.archive.org/24/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.268464/2015.268464.The-Psychology_djvu.txt (item https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.268464). Chorus (1953), *J. Abnorm. Soc. Psychol.* 48(2):313–314, https://doi.org/10.1037/h0060600 (also Krech 1948 review https://doi.org/10.1037/h0051085; Rosnow 1991 https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.46.5.484; Miller 2005 https://doi.org/10.1525/si.2005.28.4.505). Shibutani, *Improvised News* (Bobbs-Merrill, 1966), https://openlibrary.org/works/OL5104312W. Sunstein, *On Rumors* (2009/2014), https://doi.org/10.1515/9781400851225; Sunstein & Vermeule (2009), *J. Polit. Philos.* 17(2):202–227, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9760.2008.00325.x. DiFonzo & Bordia, *Rumor Psychology* (APA, 2007), https://doi.org/10.1037/11503-000. Starbird et al. (2014), iConference 2014:654–662, https://doi.org/10.9776/14308 (PDF https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/47268/bitstreams/138716/data.pdf). Friggeri et al. (2014), "Rumor Cascades," ICWSM 8(1), https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v8i1.14559 (PDF https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/download/14559/14408).

**Claims.** Allport & Postman's "basic law," verbatim: *"the amount of rumor in circulation will vary with the importance of the subject to the individuals concerned times the ambiguity of the evidence pertaining to the topic at issue. The relation between importance and ambiguity is not additive but multiplicative, for if either importance or ambiguity is zero, there is no rumor"* (pp. 33–34) **[H]**. Chorus (1953) is the standard revision, adding a "critical sense"/credulity factor **[M — citation H, content M]**. Allport himself listed weakening conditions (surveillance/penalties, fragmented populations, "rumor-wise" self-awareness; p. 35) **[H]**. The law is now widely treated as weak/contested and never adequately validated (Krech 1948; Rosnow 1991; Miller 2005) **[M — citations H, critique content largely UNVERIFIED behind paywalls]**.

Shibutani is the key alternative: rumour as *collective problem-solving/sensemaking under ambiguity*, not pathology. Verified on p. 282 by full-text phrase search: *"men caught together in an ambiguous situation attempt to construct a meaningful interpretation of it by pooling their intellectual resources"* **[H]**; the opening clause usually rendered "Rumor is a recurrent form of communication…" did **not** match the scan — **UNVERIFIED**.

**Audience as amplifier — established.** Yes, explicitly. Allport & Postman ran serial-transmission experiments with and without spectators: *"the terminal protocols were shorter and on the whole less accurate when obtained with an audience"*; *"though the rate of leveling is speeded up with an audience, the phenomenon itself is universal"* (pp. 73–74, 80) **[H]**. Modern evidence: Starbird et al. report misinformation-to-correction ratios of ~44:1, 18:1 and ~5:1, concluding *"the notion of the self-correcting crowd may be overly optimistic"*; corrections *"emerge but are muted compared with the propagation of the misinformation"*, and *"the amplitude can be exponentially different"* **[H]**. Friggeri et al.: *"rumor cascades run deeper in the social network than reshare cascades in general"*; *"large cascades are able to accumulate hundreds of Snopes comments while continuing to propagate"* **[H]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Mostly emergent, with a deliberate minority: Starbird et al. distinguish *"purposeful misinformation… and accidental misinformation, often caused by lost context"* **[H]**. Shibutani and DiFonzo & Bordia model bottom-up sensemaking.

**Fit: Partial.** High for (iii) — the audience demonstrably amplifies and correction is weak — and moderate for (ii), since rumour triggers on ambiguous events. **Does not fit:** the currency is information/belief, not physical incidents; the process is emergent; and the goal is usually ambiguity *reduction*, the opposite of sustained ambiguity.

## B) Moral panic

**Sources.** Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (MacGibbon & Kee, 1972; Routledge 2002/2011), https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203828250. Goode & Ben-Yehuda, *Moral Panics: The Social Construction of Deviance* (Blackwell, 1994); and *Annu. Rev. Sociol.* 20:149–171, https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.so.20.080194.001053. Garland (2008), *Crime, Media, Culture* 4(1):9–30, https://doi.org/10.1177/1741659007087270. McRobbie & Thornton (1995), *BJS* 46(4):559–574, https://doi.org/10.2307/591571. Altheide (2009), *CMC* 5(1):79–99, https://doi.org/10.1177/1741659008102063.

**Claims.** Cohen's definition (1972: 9), verbatim: *"Societies appear to be subject, every now and then, to periods of moral panic. A condition, episode, person or group of persons emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests; its nature is presented in a stylized and stereotypical fashion by the mass media; the moral barricades are manned by editors, bishops, politicians and other right-thinking people; socially accredited experts pronounce their diagnoses and solutions; … the condition then disappears, submerges or deteriorates…"* — verified clause-by-clause across independent full-text sources (Critcher, *Critical Readings*; Krinsky, *Ashgate Research Companion*) **[H wording; M p. 9]**. Case: Mods/Rockers at Clacton (Easter 1964), Margate (Whitsun 1964), Brighton. Roles: **folk devil**, **moral entrepreneur**, **control culture**; the **deviancy amplification spiral** descends from Wilkins (*Social Deviance*, 1964, 90–1) via Jock Young (1971) and Cohen **[H provenance; M roles]**. Goode & Ben-Yehuda's five attributes — **concern, hostility, consensus, disproportionality, volatility** — on which "the concept of moral panic *rests*" (2nd ed., Wiley-Blackwell 2009, https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444307924) **[H terms; M pagination]**. Their verified abstract: panics are constructed; concern *"need not bear a close relationship with the concrete harm"*; folk devils are *"stereotyped and classified as deviants"*; *"moral panics always leave an informal, and often an institutional, legacy"*; theories are **grassroots, elite-engineered, and interest-group** **[H]**.

**Critiques.** Cohen's 2002/2011 prefaces reportedly warn the concept is overused / becoming a throwaway phrase — exact wording **UNVERIFIED** (both IA copies lending-restricted and excluded from full-text search). Garland (2008) argues verbatim that *"some of the concept's subtlety and power has been lost as the term has become popular"* **[H]**. McRobbie & Thornton (1995) argue its assumptions (single national media, passive audience, state-centred control) no longer hold, and that folk devils are *"less marginalized than they once were"*, now *"supported and defended"* by media **[M]**. Altheide (2009) documents its migration into public discourse and loss of precision **[M]**; Murji (*Policing Drugs*, 1998) calls it a *"throwaway phrase… a 'catch all term'"* **[M]**; Joosse (*BJC* 58(4), OA https://osf.io/6xmtv/download) shows panics need not reinforce conservative morality and can be driven by charismatic entrepreneurs **[H OA]**. Net critique: elasticity — any strong public reaction can be labelled a panic, so the concept risks explaining nothing **[M]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Emergent by default: dispersed moral entrepreneurs, media and control culture amplify a minor or real event with no central engineer. Goode & Ben-Yehuda formalise this and treat "elite-engineered" panics as a rarer minority variant; Hall et al. (*Policing the Crisis*, 1978) push toward state/ruling-class orchestration **[H/M]**.

**Fit: Partial.** The amplification spiral plus "moral entrepreneur" models how an ambiguous trigger and institutional reaction produce disproportionate disruption, and the elite-engineered variant licenses engineering. **Does not fit:** moral panic requires a *folk devil* and normative/deviance threat, not an ambiguous physical hazard; the literature is overwhelmingly emergent; and critics warn against over-application.

## C) Availability cascades

**Source.** Kuran & Sunstein (1999), "Availability Cascades and Risk Regulation," *Stanford Law Review* 51(4):683–768, https://doi.org/10.2307/1229439. OA: https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/public_law_and_legal_theory/37 ; SSRN https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=138144. Journal/volume/pages confirmed by Kuran's own CV.

**Claims (verbatim abstract) [H].** *"An availability cascade is a self-reinforcing process of collective belief formation by which an expressed perception triggers a chain reaction that gives the perception increasing plausibility through its rising availability in public discourse."* Mechanism: *"a combination of informational and reputational motives: Individuals endorse the perception partly by learning from the apparent beliefs of others and partly by distorting their public responses in the interest of maintaining social acceptance."* **"Availability entrepreneurs"** are theirs: *"activists who manipulate the content of public discourse—strive to trigger availability cascades likely to advance their agendas."* Campaigns *"may yield social benefits, but sometimes they bring harm"*; remedies include insulating civil servants and a scientific database **[H]**.

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Explicitly dual: informational/reputational cascades are emergent; availability entrepreneurs deliberately trigger them **[H]**.

**Critiques.** No single canonical refutation located; critique is diffuse — the model is hard to falsify, usually demonstrated by case study, "availability entrepreneur" is a residual category assertable post hoc, and it underplays organized interests and media incentives **[M–L; partly UNVERIFIED]**. Kuran & Sunstein extended it in "Controlling Availability Cascades," in Sunstein (ed.), *Behavioral Law and Economics* (2000), https://doi.org/10.1017/cbo9781139175197.016.

**Fit: High for (i)/(iii), Partial overall.** Closest match to "deliberate amplification by agenda-driven actors whose target's own beliefs and need for social acceptance supply the escalation." **Does not fit:** the currency is availability/belief, not physical incidents; remedies are epistemic, not operational.

## D) Social Amplification of Risk Framework (SARF)

**Sources.** Kasperson, Renn, Slovic, Brown, Emel, Goble, Kasperson & Ratick (1988), *Risk Analysis* 8(2):177–187, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x — record https://commons.clarku.edu/faculty_idce/195 ; full text https://web.archive.org/web/20241010151234/https://elib.uni-stuttgart.de/bitstream/11682/7238/1/ren27.pdf. Pidgeon, Kasperson & Slovic (eds.) (2003), *The Social Amplification of Risk*, https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511550461. Kasperson, Webler, Ram & Sutton (2022), *Risk Analysis* 42(7):1367–1380, https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.13926 (OA https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360138/).

**Claims (verbatim primary) [H].** *"Social amplification of risk denotes the phenomenon by which information processes, institutional structures, social-group behavior, and individual responses shape the social experience of risk, thereby contributing to risk consequences."* Amplification occurs *"in the transfer of information about the risk, and in the response mechanisms of society"*; signals pass through *"individual and social amplification stations, including the scientist who communicates the risk assessment, the news media, cultural groups, interpersonal networks, and others"* (seven stations). The metaphor: *"amplification denotes the process of intensifying or attenuating signals during the transmission of information from an information source, to intermediate transmitters, and finally to a receiver."* Ripples: *"The ripples spread outward, first encompassing the directly affected victims… then touching the next higher institutional level (a company or an agency), and, in more extreme cases, reaching other parts of the industry."* The authors self-limit it as *"a fledgling conceptual framework"* **[H]**.

**Revisions.** Pidgeon et al. (2003) add metatheory, organizational amplification, trust, a media rethink and policy analysis **[H TOC, M content]**. The 2022 review surveys 50+ post-2003 studies, foregrounds social media as a station, and concludes SARF is *"not a predictive theory of social outcomes"* but an organizational aid **[H]**.

**Critiques.** They began in the **same issue**: Rayner, "Muddling Through Metaphors to Maturity" (1988), https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01172.x, plus Rip, Rappaport and Svenson commentaries **[H existence; M content]**. The 2022 review concedes *"Some critics suggest that SARF cannot be tested thus, the framework is useful, at most, as a post hoc analysis"* and debates *"whether SARF is limited as a framework and a metaphor and is not a theory"* **[H]** — i.e. descriptive-not-predictive and vague-metaphor charges are acknowledged by its architects.

**Deliberate/adversarial amplification — key negative finding.** Exact-phrase full-text and title searches found **no established connection** between SARF and deliberate/adversarial amplification; "adversarial social amplification of risk," "deliberate amplification of risk," "weaponised/weaponized social amplification," "intentional amplification of risk" returned no substantive hits, and the 2022 review's survey contains no adversarial framing **[M–H negative; search tools partly bot-blocked, not exhaustive]**. Adjacent only: Fjaeran & Aven (2021) propose *intentionally injecting* amplification to counter attenuation (defensive), https://doi.org/10.1080/13669877.2019.1687579 ; Ram & Webler (2022) document advocacy strategies, https://doi.org/10.1111/risa.13902 . Deliberate amplification is theorized in disinformation/hybrid-warfare work but **not bridged to SARF** **[M]** — a genuine gap the hypothesis could claim.

**Fit: High for mechanism, Partial for deliberate.** Best model of (iii): signals pass through "stations" that co-produce behavioural and secondary impacts from a modest trigger. **Does not fit:** explicitly emergent and non-predictive, with no adversary, and perception-centred rather than incident-centred.

## E) Information laundering

**Sources.** Peterson, *Russia and the Information Revolution* (RAND MG-422, 2005), p. 84, https://www.rand.org/pubs/monographs/MG422.html (PDF https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/monographs/2005/RAND_MG422.pdf). Klein, *A Space for Hate* (Litwin, 2010); Klein (2012), *Communication Theory* 22(4):427–448, https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2885.2012.01415.x. Puschmann, Ausserhofer, Maan & Hametner (2016), ICWSM 10(2), https://doi.org/10.1609/icwsm.v10i2.14847. DFRLab, https://dfrlab.org/2017/01/12/three-thousand-fake-tanks/.

**Claims.** Move content through intermediaries (front groups, fringe sites, fake personas, legitimate outlets) so provenance is obscured and borrowed credibility accrues. RAND (2005): *"'information laundering'—posting rumors or gossip, which then would be picked up by the mainstream broadcast and print media as legitimate stories"* **[H]**. Puschmann et al., following Klein: *"Information laundering… is the successful redressing of racist ideology in the guise of legitimate information, achieved by relying on the same forms, appearances and labels that give traditional information their societal legitimacy"* **[H]**. DFRLab describes a story launched by a dubious site, picked up by fringe sites, then repeated by RIA Novosti citing those sites — *"a classic case of disinformation laundering"* **[H]**.

**Origin.** Earlier and looser than assumed: earliest verified use, Ed Ayres, *God's Last Offer* (1999); earliest defence/IO-adjacent use, RAND 2005; academic theory, Klein (2010/2012) **[H/M]**. Usage is contested: incompatible senses for PR, pharma ghost-journals, front groups, Russian kompromat, racist propaganda **[H]**. **UNVERIFIED:** 2003/2007 US military IO-doctrine usage (DTIC found none); any Matusitz usage; a "cyber-laundering" lineage (financial-crime metaphor).

**Deliberate vs emergent.** Predominantly **deliberate engineering** (Klein, RAND, DFRLab) **[H]**; Puschmann et al.'s source-overlap operationalisation captures partly emergent systemic amplification **[M]**.

**Fit: High for (i), Partial overall.** Models deliberate, cheap, intermediary-mediated amplification and maps onto (iii). **Does not fit:** the currency is information provenance, not physical incidents; it stops at credibility transfer and does not model operational disruption; definitions are too loose to operationalise without care.

## Cross-cutting verdict

| Literature | Deliberate? | Audience amplifies? | Physical incidents? | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rumour theory (Allport; Shibutani; Starbird) | Emergent (minor deliberate) | **Yes [H]** | Partial (ambiguous information) | **Partial** |
| Moral panic (Cohen; Goode & Ben-Yehuda) | Both (elite-engineered) | Yes (media/institutions) | No (deviance) | **Partial** |
| Availability cascades (Kuran & Sunstein) | **Yes [H]** | Yes (belief/reputation) | No | **High/Partial** |
| SARF (Kasperson et al.) | **No — explicitly not** | **Yes — stations/ripples [H]** | **Yes — risk events [H]** | **High/Partial** |
| Information laundering | **Yes [H]** | Yes (credibility transfer) | No | **High/Partial** |

**Bottom line.** "Rumour/risk theory treats the audience as amplifier" is **well supported [H]**. No literature models an autonomous, scalable, agentic amplifier, so the AI-engine novelty is **not contradicted**. "Ambiguous physical incident" targeting is **only partly covered** (SARF: risk events but no adversary; rumour: ambiguity but about information). The adversarial bridge is **absent from SARF [M–H negative]** and present only in availability-cascade and information-laundering work. The strongest defensible novelty is the **synthesis**: deliberate amplification (availability cascades, information laundering) of ambiguous physical risk events (SARF's domain) via autonomous agents, with the audience supplying the disruptive effect (rumour theory/moral panic). **Residual uncertainty:** Cohen's exact 2002 preface wording, Garland/McRobbie-Thornton/Chorus/Miller/Krech/Rayner contents are paywalled or UNVERIFIED; search engines were largely bot-blocked, so the "adversarial SARF" negative is strong but not exhaustive.
