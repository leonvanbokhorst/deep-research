The Bottle on the Shelf Still Says the Same Thing It Did Before
Almost a year after federal officials announced they had begun changing the safety label on acetaminophen to reflect a possible link to autism and ADHD , the label has not changed.
The current federal record for Tylenol Regular Strength , filed by Kenvue Brands LLC and posted on the National Library of Medicine's DailyMed database, carries one sentence about pregnancy in its warnings: if pregnant or breastfeeding, ask a health professional before use. That is the same wording the product carried before September 2025.
The detail that matters is the timing. This is version 13 of that label, revised May 27, 2026, eight months after the announcement. Version 12 is dated November 2024. The manufacturer filed an updated label in the interval, and no neurodevelopmental language appears in it. Generic acetaminophen labels and other Tylenol formulations on the same database read the same way.
For a pregnant woman deciding in a pharmacy aisle whether to treat a fever, that gap is the practical fact. She may have heard a warning described on television that does not exist on the package in her hand.
Monograph Rules Set a Slower Path Than a Press Conference Suggests
The FDA said on Sept. 22, 2025, that it had initiated the process for a label change and had sent a letter to physicians nationwide. In that same announcement, the agency stated that "a causal relationship has not been established" and noted contrary findings in the literature.
Initiating a process is not the same as finishing one. Most over-the-counter acetaminophen is sold under OTC Monograph M013, and monograph labeling cannot be rewritten by announcement. Under the framework Congress created in 2020, changes run through an administrative order process: the agency issues a proposed order, the public gets a comment period, the agency reviews those comments and issues a final order, and final orders remain subject to dispute resolution, an administrative hearing, and judicial review.
There is a faster lane. The agency can issue an interim final order that takes effect before comment when a labeling change is reasonably expected to mitigate a significant or unreasonable risk of a serious adverse event. It has not used that lane here.
The only agency-initiated proposed order currently amending Monograph M013 is OTC000035, on severe skin reactions , issued in June 2024. Nothing comparable has been issued on neurodevelopment. Two citizen petitions are pending instead. The first, filed by the Informed Consent Action Network on the same day as the White House event, asks the agency to amend the monograph to warn about neurodevelopmental risk in pregnancy. A second petition, filed that November, seeks similar language extending to use in young children.
Both drew opposition. Kenvue asked the agency to deny the petition , arguing the change is unsupported by the evidence and would depart from the agency's own longstanding position. The Consumer Healthcare Products Association argued the petition route sidesteps the statutory process altogether. "Congress established a clear process for OTC label changes," said Scott Furness, the trade group's senior vice president for regulatory and scientific affairs, in its public statement .
The Evidence Base Grew While the Label Stayed Still
Research published during the intervening year has pushed in one direction.
The largest recent analysis came from the University of Hong Kong and appeared in JAMA Internal Medicine in June. Researchers examined electronic health records covering 708,020 mother-child pairs from 2001 through 2023, then narrowed to families in which one sibling was exposed to the drug before birth, and another was not. That design accounts for the genetics and household environment that ordinary observational studies cannot separate from the medication itself.
The sibling matched analysis , which covered 124,333 children for autism and 97,285 for ADHD, found no association between prenatal exposure and either condition. The hazard ratio was 1.00 for autism and 1.01 for ADHD, and the result held regardless of trimester, dose or pattern of use. The authors concluded that positive signals in conventional studies are likely explained by residual familial confounding.
That study joins similar sibling comparison work, including a 2024 analysis of roughly 2.5 million Swedish children that also found no increased risk. The industry trade group puts the combined sibling-controlled evidence at more than 2.7 million children.
Two limits are worth stating plainly. These are observational studies, not randomized trials, and no such design eliminates every source of confounding. What the sibling method does well is test whether an association survives once shared family factors are removed, and so far it has not.
The evidence the agency cited pointed the other way and was smaller. It included a Nurses' Health Study II analysis of 8,856 children, which examined ADHD only and relied on recalled, self-reported use, and a Boston Birth Cohort study of 996 mother-infant pairs that measured acetaminophen byproducts in umbilical cord blood. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists objected at the time. "Today's announcement by HHS is not backed by the full body of scientific evidence," ACOG president Steven Fleischman said in the group's statement.
Pregnant Patients Are Making Choices Inside the Gap
Behavior appears to have shifted regardless of what the label says. Researchers at Brown University and Mass General Brigham, writing in The Lancet , found that acetaminophen orders for pregnant emergency department patients fell about 10 percent in the weeks after the September 2025 briefing, with the steepest drop in the first month. Leucovorin prescriptions for children aged five to 17 rose 71 percent over the same stretch. Emergency department acetaminophen ordering trended back toward baseline by early December; leucovorin prescribing did not.
That decline carries its own risk. Untreated high fever in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes including preterm birth, and acetaminophen remains the over-the-counter option obstetric groups recommend, because aspirin and other anti-inflammatory drugs carry documented fetal effects. The agency's own physician letter acknowledged that acetaminophen is the safest over-the-counter analgesic and antipyretic available in pregnancy.
Nobody should change a medication based on a news article. The reasonable step for someone pregnant or planning to be is to ask the clinician managing the pregnancy what fits their situation, and to raise a fever or persistent pain rather than wait it out. It is also worth checking whether other products in the cabinet contain acetaminophen, since combining them is the established liver risk already on the label.
Several outcomes remain open. The agency has not said whether it will grant or deny either petition, has not issued a proposed order on neurodevelopment, and has not published a timeline. If it does move, the earliest public signal would be a proposed administrative order posted to the OTC monographs portal, followed by a public comment window. Until then, the label is the label.

Key Questions Answered
What did the FDA actually announce? On Sept. 22, 2025, the agency said it had initiated the process for a label change on acetaminophen and had written to physicians. It also said a causal relationship has not been established.
Has the label changed? No. The current federal label record for Tylenol Regular Strength, revised May 27, 2026, still carries only the standard instruction to ask a health professional before use if pregnant or breastfeeding.
Why is it taking this long? Over-the-counter acetaminophen is governed by a drug monograph. Changing it requires a proposed administrative order, a public comment period, and a final order, with rights to appeal.
Is there a faster option? Yes. The agency can issue an interim final order effective before comment when a labeling change would mitigate a significant risk of a serious adverse event. It has not done so here.
What does the newest research show? A sibling-matched study drawn from 708,020 mother-child pairs in Hong Kong found no association with autism or ADHD, consistent with earlier sibling comparison work from Sweden.
Does that settle the question? Not entirely. These are observational studies, not randomized trials. What they show is that the association does not survive once shared family factors are accounted for.
What should pregnant patients do? Do not start or stop any medication based on news coverage. Discuss fever and pain management with the clinician handling the pregnancy, and check other products for acetaminophen to avoid doubling the dose.
